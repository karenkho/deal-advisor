Synthetic guidance derived only from the structure and tone of proposals in general. Generic, rewritten content: no client names, amounts, dates or people. Not indexed with the estimation corpus.

## Executive summary

Open with what the client wants to achieve and why it matters to the business, then state in one or two sentences what the vendor proposes. Say how the work will be delivered, and close with what the client gets at the end. Stay under two short paragraphs. Do not list features or commercial details here.

## Client requirements

Restate the client's needs in the client's own terms, grouped by business area. This section shows that the vendor read and understood the request. Each item is short, faithful to the Terms of Reference and free of solution detail.

## Proposed solution

Describe the solution as a whole: the main capabilities it brings together, who uses them and how they fit the client's way of working. Explain the approach in plain language and name the benefits the client asked for. Avoid product marketing and avoid promises the scope does not support.

## Functional and non-functional scope

Set out what will be built. Functional items are grouped by module with one clear statement each. Non-functional items cover quality targets, hosting, security and compatibility, stated as the client stated them. Anything still to be confirmed is pointed out rather than hidden.

## Project deliverables

List what the client receives: planning documents, requirements and design documents, the deployed system, test results, manuals, training and turnover materials. Each entry is a concrete item the client can check and sign off.

## Technical architecture

Summarize the architecture at reference level: application layers, services, data store, integration approach, environments and how the system scales and is secured. Explain why the design suits the requirement. Leave low-level design to the project itself.

## Assumptions and exclusions

State the conditions the proposal relies on and what is outside it. Typical topics are the client's product owner and decision-making, the working arrangement between the teams, how new features are handled through change requests, sign-off of deliverables, third-party dependencies, who runs user acceptance testing and who operates the system after turnover. Exclusions are listed plainly.

## Delivery methodology

Explain the agile, iterative approach: short time-boxed cycles, regular demonstrations, continuous feedback from the client and incremental releases. Describe quality practices across the cycle and how changes to scope are controlled. Describe how AI assistants and agents support delivery under human supervision, with people reviewing, integrating and approving every output.

## Project team

Present the roles the vendor assigns and what each is responsible for, such as project manager, technical architect, business analyst, developers, quality engineers and infrastructure engineer. Mention what is expected from the client's side. Show roles and their allocation, never individual names or internal costs.

## Timeline and milestones

Show the phases from mobilization to go-live in a simple table, with the main milestones and what the client must provide for each. Note that dates assume timely client decisions and access.

## Commercial pricing and payment milestones

Present the total price in a clear table, with tax shown separately, followed by the payment schedule tied to milestones. Keep this section factual. Show only the price and the payment terms, never how the price was built up.

## Warranty and support

Explain what the warranty covers, how long it lasts, how defects are reported and corrected, and what falls outside it. Any further support is described as a separate option.

## Terms requiring management review

For internal review before the draft is sent: list the terms that differ from the client's request or still need a decision. Each entry is one neutral line. Remove this section once management has decided.
